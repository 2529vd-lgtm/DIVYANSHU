Whether the Taj Mahal is a symbol of love or not, a far bigger question is: when we look at it, whom do we remember, and whom do we forget?
  
Court historians documented Shah Jahan’s grief in great detail: hair turning gray, a bent posture, a year-long isolation. Perhaps this sorrow was genuine. Yet, from the pages of those very historians emerges another picture: a girl betrothed at the age of fourteen, who remained almost continuously pregnant for eighteen years, who lost half her children, and who, even during her final pregnancy, was stationed in a military camp.**She died after thirty hours of labor.** Her own words are nowhere to be found in history. We know her solely through the eyes of the man who erected the world's most beautiful monument over her grave.

So, was it love? Perhaps in that era, love and power were so deeply entangled that separating them is nearly impossible. Giving birth to an heir in a royal household was not merely a personal matter; it was integral to the empire. Calling it just a "symbol of love" is an incomplete truth, while reducing her to a "childbearing machine" erases her intellect and her influence in the imperial court. The truth likely lies somewhere in between, and that is precisely the space least explored. 

Then, there are the **20,000 workers**. Amanat Khan's name is engraved beneath the dome; Ustad Ahmad Lahori’s name is recorded in history books. But the boy who hauled wet mud from the Yamuna from morning until evening, the woman who baked flatbreads in the inn, the man who fell from the scaffolding and never rose again—they have no names. The story of hands being severed is a myth, but that history severed their names is an undeniable truth.

Today, we assume we have moved past that era. Yet, the deaths of 38 construction workers every day and young girls earning a mere one rupee per piece just beyond the walls of the Taj remind us that only the tools have evolved, not the mindset. Even now, we capture photographs of the building, not of the ones who built it.   There is no doubt that the Taj Mahal is a wonder. But the next time you stand before it, perhaps pause for a moment for the woman whose grave it is, and for a moment for the hands that raised it.*** Beneath the sheen of the marble lie buried sweat, blood, and silence. That, perhaps, is the true wonder***
