Some foods reached the whole country. Others stayed where they began. I don't think taste had much to do with it.

Get off a train in a city you've never been to, at seven in the morning, hungry, a few days into being away from home. You won't have to look far. There's a dosa stall within a hundred steps, a momo cart a little further on, and if you'd rather stay put, a pizza can find you in half an hour. What you won't find, anywhere in that city, is the thing you're actually craving: your mother's litti, or her dal baati, or the thekua she packs when you leave, or whatever dish from your own home you've known by name since you were small.

It's an odd thing when you say it plainly. In one country, one kind of food reached every street corner and another kind stopped at its own doorstep. Why?

Picture two plates. On the first, put what you can eat in nearly any Indian city today: dosa, momos, pizza, burger, chowmein, samosa. On the second, put the food that is dearest to a great many of us and hardest to find outside our own kitchens: litti chokha, dal baati churma, thekua, anything made of sattu, a chutney made from the red ants that tribal families gather in the forest, and a long list of others I haven't the space to name. I can't see a gap in taste between the two plates. Anyone who has had litti chokha with a spoon of ghee on top asks for it again, and nobody forgets hot dal baati broken open over churma. What separates the plates is that one of them found a road and the other didn't.

The first plate's roads are easy to trace. Tradition ties dosa to Udupi in Karnataka, and cooks from there opened hotels in other cities until it became a breakfast the whole country shares. Momos came from Tibet, carried by refugees who arrived after 1959 and later by Nepali workers, who sold them on the streets of cities like Delhi. Today the momo counts among Delhi's most common street foods, and Dolma Aunty, who sells them there, now runs three shops with more than twenty people working for her. Pizza needed neither a community nor a lane. It had a company. Domino's opened its 2,000th Indian store in June 2024, spread over 421 cities. Put the three side by side and the pattern is plain enough. Each time, someone built a way to sell the food, whether a hotel, a cart or a chain of stores, and the food travelled exactly as far as that way went.

The second plate has had no shortage of love. Litti is cooked in a great many homes, week after week. But we have kept it for our own kitchens, for festivals and weddings, and the work of building a road for it, a shop, a stall, a brand, has mostly not been done. Nobody chose this. It happened quietly, one family at a time, while the first plate grew street by street. I suspect part of the reason is how easily a food turns into a product. Makhana is dry and keeps in a packet, so it ended up as a snack on shelves. Litti has to be eaten fresh and roasted over fire, and dal baati has to reach the table hot, which makes both harder to box up and sell.

There's a less comfortable question underneath that one, though. Somewhere along the way, have we started treating our own food as something for home, not quite good enough to sell or show off? Most of us order a pizza without a flicker of hesitation. How many of us would hesitate before naming our own dish to a stranger?

None of this is the government's fault. Governments don't spread food, people and markets do, and it would be unfair to ask a ministry to do what a neighbourhood cook is meant to. Still, a little help isn't nothing. A GI tag gives a food an official identity: makhana received one in 2022, and the red ant chutney of Mayurbhanj in Odisha followed in January 2024. Several state bhawan canteens in Delhi are open to the public, and a fair can give a small seller a stall. Social media does something similar, only faster. One video can make a cart famous overnight. But that is light, not a kitchen, and the kitchen still has to be built by us and by small entrepreneurs.

If those roads did open, the people who gain most would be farmers. Behind every plate there is a field. Behind dosa are the farmers who grow rice and lentils, behind pizza wheat, tomatoes and milk, behind dal baati wheat, lentils and ghee, behind sattu chickpeas, behind makhana ponds, and behind red ant chutney a forest and the tribal families who live in it. The wider our plate gets, the wider the range of crops people ask for, and the more different kinds of farmers the money reaches. Every farmer in this country grows something different, and every food we come to love ends at some field. In Mayurbhanj, a man who gathers the ants told a reporter that a kilo used to fetch about 100 rupees and now sells for 600 to 700. That is one person's account, not a figure for a whole community, but it shows what recognition can do.

I should be honest about the limits. That gain doesn't arrive on its own, and it matters just as much that the money reaches the person whose labour is on the plate. Nor is every food meant for the market. For a tribal community, a food tradition holds more than flavour. It carries knowledge of the forest, the seasons and a livelihood, so how far to take it to market is a decision for that community to make first.

There's another reason this matters, and it has little to do with money. Think of a hostel room where young men from different places live together. The first thing shared is almost always a tiffin from home. The whole country sits in that box. Now imagine dosa, litti, momos and dal baati selling side by side in a single lane. People don't divide over food like that, they taste. Someone who has tasted a neighbour's food usually ends up learning the neighbour's name, and the line between "ours" and "theirs" gets a little thinner. Both plates belong to this country, and so do the people who eat from them.

So the question comes back to us. Asking the government and entrepreneurs for capital, packaging and branding is fair enough, but it can't be where the thinking stops. When did you last feed a neighbour, a friend from the hostel or a colleague from the office the food of your home? You can start this week. Send a neighbour something from your kitchen at the next festival, and tell them the story behind it. Find a stall serving food from somewhere else and ask the person behind the counter what it is and how it's made. Buy sattu and makhana straight from the people who produce them when you can. Post a photo or video of your region's food, with its name and the name of whoever made it. And if you're ever in Delhi, eat once at a state bhawan canteen.

Go back, now, to that strange city at seven in the morning. This time there's dosa within a hundred steps, and momos, and a cart where litti is roasting over coals with a stranger standing in front of it, asking, "What is that?" On that day our food won't only fill a stomach. It will introduce us.
