

**From 160 to 3,700 Kilometres:**

**More Embankments, More Floods**


In September 2026, the Ganga at Patna's Gandhi Ghat crossed its highest flood level ever recorded. Around five million people were hit by the floods. A man in Saran district put it simply: floods are our fate; we face them every year. For as long as I can remember, Bihar has flooded. Sometimes the flood is small, sometimes big, but it comes every single year. We have built great bridges over seas and rivers. Humans have walked on the Moon, and our spacecraft have reached Mars. So why, almost 80 years after independence, can we still not stop floods in our own state? I come from South Bihar, where the problem is the opposite. The Ganga divides Bihar in two. The north drowns every year, while the south often struggles with drought. There have been years when 13 districts in the north were under water while 10 districts in the south, such as Gaya, Aurangabad and Jamui, were reeling from drought. Bihar's problem, in other words, is not water. It is the management of water.

**Life Under Water**

Go and see a flood-hit area for yourself. Those who own a two-storey house move upstairs, along with their cattle. Those who don't end up on a highway, a raised road or a government school. Even sitting in our own rooms, we get bitten by mosquitoes. Imagine how many snakes and venomous creatures swim in that floodwater. When the water recedes, disease spreads. Since 1979, about 9,500 people have died in Bihar's floods, and the real number is probably higher. Women and children bear the heaviest burden. Camps and roadsides have no toilets and no security at night. Schools remain relief camps for weeks, and children's education is washed away with the water.

**More Embankments, More Floods**

It is often assumed that the British built big dams to stop floods. The truth is the reverse. In 1855 they built embankments on the Damodar river; the water rose dangerously between them, and by 1869 the British themselves had demolished about 32 km of those embankments. At the 1937 Patna Flood Conference, Bihar's Chief Engineer, Hall, stated plainly that embankments block the flow of water and make floods worse. That is why, at independence, Bihar had only 160 km of embankments. The British were not our well-wishers, but they did learn from their mistake. After independence, we forgot that lesson. In 1954, Bihar had 160 km of embankments and 2.5 million hectares of flood-prone land. By 2016, embankments had grown to 3,731 km, and the flood-prone area had swelled to nearly 7.3 million hectares. Today, 73% of Bihar's land is at risk of flooding.

Earlier this silt spread across the fields; now it settles inside the river, between the embankments. The bed of the Kosi has risen by 12 to 15 feet. When the Kosi embankment breached at Kusaha in 2008, the river was carrying less water than normal. The flood came not from the river but from the broken embankment. As early as 1979, a Planning Commission evaluation admitted that the Kosi embankments had worsened the problem, yet in 1987-88 they were raised by another two metres. The most forgotten people are those who live inside the embankments. When the Kosi embankments were built, about 304 villages were left between them. Those outside were promised safety; those inside were handed a flood every year.

**Fertile Land, Empty Fields, Packed Trains**

North Bihar's soil is among the most fertile in the country, yet about 3 million of the state's 9.36 million hectares of cultivable land is at risk of flooding. Between 1979 and 2006, an average of 7.7 million people were affected by floods every year. After the 2017 floods, the state asked the Centre for Rs 7,636 crore in assistance. The government spends roughly Rs 1,000 crore a year on relief, money that builds nothing new and only patches up old losses. The real cost does not even show up in these figures. A farmer who knows his field may go under in August will not invest in good seeds or machinery. When the crop is lost, one man from the household boards a train to Delhi, Punjab or Surat. Studies have found the highest migration in the flood-hit regions of Kosi, Darbhanga, Tirhut and Purnia. Villagers in many places say that large-scale migration began after the 1987 flood. Compensation arrives late, so families sink into debt. Astonishingly, there is no reliable account of how much floods have cost Bihar since independence. In the Central Water Commission's 2019 records, the entry against Bihar's crop losses simply reads 'not reported'. If we do not even count the loss, how will we ever plan to prevent it?

**The Flood Business**

Every year the same scene is repeated. The Chief Minister or Prime Minister circles overhead in a helicopter. Affected families receive a few thousand rupees, Rs 7,000 this year. Relief packets are dropped from helicopters, but nobody knows who got them and who didn't. Within 15 days the water recedes, and so does the discussion. In Bihar, floods have become less a disaster and more an industry. The same politicians, the same middlemen, the same contractors. Embankments are built, they break, and a fresh repair contract is issued. Why would a system that profits from floods want to stop them? Lack of money is not the whole truth either. After 2008, the World Bank provided 220 million dollars in 2010 and another 250 million dollars in 2015. The completion report of the second project shows that 72 million dollars of it could not be spent and was cancelled. People drown every year, and the money meant for them goes back.


**If There Were No Floods**

Imagine what would change if floods in North Bihar were brought under control. A farmer whose field drowns every August could grow two or three crops a year and invest confidently in good seeds, fertiliser and machinery. The fertile silt from Nepal would become a blessing rather than a curse. With work available in the village, young men would not have to catch a train to another state every year. Roads, bridges and schools would not have to be rebuilt year after year. The roughly Rs 1,000 crore that goes into relief every year could be spent on hospitals, colleges and industry. And the water that wreaks havoc in the north, if stored and managed, could reach the thirsty fields of the south. Flood control is not just disaster management; it is the biggest key to Bihar's development.

**What Is the Way Forward**

A changing climate is raising the stakes. In the last 10 years, Bihar has had below-normal rainfall seven times and above-normal rainfall three times. Drought in one place, record-breaking downpours within days in another. If we do not prepare today, tomorrow's floods will be bigger. There is no shortage of solutions, only of implementation. In 1980, the Rashtriya Barh Ayog (National Flood Commission) made 207 recommendations, including a legal ban on construction in floodplains. In 2017, the CAG found they had not been implemented. In 2016, the Chitale Committee recommended keeping an annual account of the silt entering the river, planting trees in the Nepal catchment, and keeping floodplains and lakes free so that water can spread out. The world has shown that it can be done. After the floods of 1993 and 1995, the Netherlands decided that raising embankments ever higher was not the answer. Under its 'Room for the River' programme, 34 projects were built at a cost of 2.3 billion euros, moving embankments back and digging additional channels for the river. In the next major flood, not a single life was lost. Bangladesh put its strength into early warning. Its Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre can now predict floods up to 10 days in advance. Studies suggest timely warnings can cut deaths and losses by more than a third. Google ran the very first pilot of its flood-warning system in Patna in 2018. The cheapest and quickest step for Bihar is to share rainfall and river-level data with Nepal every hour, and to deliver warnings to every village in its own language. The Kosi High Dam has been under discussion since 1937, and the Kosi-Mechi link has been approved. The pace of both depends on honest dialogue with Nepal.

**Conclusion**

Bihar does not need relief every year; it needs an honest, long-term plan. For a country whose spacecraft have reached Mars, controlling floods is not impossible. What is missing is not technology or money, but will and memory. As long as we treat floods as a 15-day news story, next year will arrive just the same, with the same helicopter and the same Rs 7,000.
